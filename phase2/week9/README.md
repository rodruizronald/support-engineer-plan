## Module 9 — Week 9: The Network as Hardware — Packets, Addresses, and Ports

### Weekly Objective

Internalize that the network is the **fourth component from Week 1** — real hardware with a queue at each end — and that reaching another machine takes three separate things: an **address** (which machine), a **route** (how to get there), and a **port** (which program on the far end). See, in your own machine's tables and your own running Python code, that data travels as **packets** that may be dropped or reordered, that a **socket is a Week 5 file descriptor** with an address attached, and that "the network is slow" is really three different claims — latency, bandwidth, and loss — with three different causes.

### Brief Context

The network is not a pipe, and it is not a phone line. It is hardware — a **NIC** (Wi-Fi or Ethernet) at each end, and switches and routers in between — and the unit it moves is not your file, your request, or even your byte: it is the **packet**. A packet is a small chunk of data (typically at most ~1500 bytes, the interface's **MTU**) carrying a header that says where it came from and where it's going. Nobody sends a 1 GB file; you send roughly 700,000 packets and hope. And "hope" is the right word, because the internet is **best-effort** by design: any packet may be dropped, delayed, duplicated, or arrive out of order, and nothing in the network layer promises otherwise. That looks like a defect until you notice it's the reason the internet scales — the routers stay dumb and fast, and the *endpoints* are left to notice a missing packet and ask again. That endpoint bookkeeping is TCP, which is Weeks 11–12; this week is the layer underneath it.

Getting a packet to the right machine takes **two kinds of address**, and confusing them is the source of a lot of muddled debugging. A **MAC address** identifies a piece of hardware on one local link, and it is rewritten at every hop. An **IP address** identifies a machine's position in the global network, and it survives the whole journey. Your machine keeps a **routing table** whose job is one decision per packet: is this destination on my own link — in which case, deliver it directly — or does it belong to the rest of the world, in which case hand it to the **default gateway** and let it worry? The **subnet mask** (the `/24` in CIDR notation) is what draws that line. From there the packet travels **hop by hop**, each router decrementing a **TTL** counter that exists so a routing loop dies instead of circling forever — and `traceroute` is a clever abuse of exactly that counter. One more wrinkle you will meet constantly: your laptop almost certainly has a **private** address (`192.168.x.x`, `10.x.x.x`) that is not unique on the internet and cannot be reached from it. **NAT** on your router rewrites your packets to use its own public address on the way out and reverses the translation on the way back, which is why a web server logs an address you have never seen on your own machine — and why *inbound* connections to your laptop are the thing NAT quietly broke.

An address gets you to the machine; a **port** picks which program on it. Ports are handed out by the OS, and a single connection is identified not by a port but by the whole **four-tuple**: source address, source port, destination address, destination port. That is why two programs can't both `listen()` on port 8080 (you get "Address already in use") while one web server happily holds ten thousand simultaneous connections on port 443 — every one of them differs in the client's half of the tuple. It's also why your *outbound* request occupies a port too: the OS picks a high-numbered **ephemeral** one for you. And because the OS owns all of this, you reach it exactly the way you reached the disk in Week 6 — through a **file descriptor**. A socket *is* a file descriptor with an address bound to it; the small integer your Python code prints from `fileno()` is the same integer `lsof` shows you. Week 5's middleman is still the middleman, only now the other end of the pipe is another continent. The one detail with outsized consequences is **which address you bind to**: `127.0.0.1` means "only this machine can reach me," `0.0.0.0` means "any interface, including the network" — and that single choice is the whole explanation of "it works on my machine and nothing else can connect."

Finally, "the network is slow" needs to be broken into three numbers that have nothing to do with each other. **Latency** is how long a round trip takes — bounded below by the speed of light and inflated by queueing. **Bandwidth** is how much data fits through per second. **Loss** (and its cousin, jitter) is what fraction of packets never make it. Buying more bandwidth does nothing for latency, and this is where Week 1's hierarchy comes back with force: a round trip to a server is 10–100 ms, which is roughly a **million times** slower than a RAM access. So twenty requests made one after another at 50 ms each cost a full second of wall clock, no matter how fat the link is — which is why "chatty" is the most common performance bug in a distributed system, and why "we upgraded the connection and it's still slow" is a ticket you will genuinely receive.

None of this is academic. "It's up but nothing can reach it" — bound to loopback, or a firewall silently dropping. "The IP in our logs is not the customer's" — NAT, or a proxy (Week 14's `X-Forwarded-For`). "It's slow only for our EU customers" — latency, geography, routing. "It works for everyone except one office" — loss, MTU, or somebody's middlebox. This week you build the map before the protocols arrive: what an address is, what a route is, what a port is, and what your own machine's tables will tell you when you ask them properly.

### Research Questions

Answer each in your own words in `solutions/answers.md`. Don't copy-paste definitions — explain them as if to a friend.

#### Part A — The wire, the addresses, and the route

1. What is a **packet**, and what's the difference between its **header** and its **payload**? What is an interface's **MTU** (typically ~1500 bytes), and what does that imply about transferring a 1 GB file? The network is **best-effort**: name the things it is allowed to do to your packet, and explain why designing it that way — instead of guaranteeing delivery — was the right call.
2. A packet carries two kinds of address. What does a **MAC address** identify, and what does an **IP address** identify? Which of the two is rewritten at every hop and which survives the whole trip, what is **ARP** for, and why does a machine need both?
3. What does a **router** actually do with a packet that arrives? Explain what's in your machine's **routing table**, what the **default gateway** is, and the one decision the table exists to make. Then explain what a **hop** is, how `traceroute` abuses **TTL** to reveal the path, and why the return path can differ from the outbound one.
4. Compare **IPv4** and **IPv6**, and explain what a **subnet mask** / CIDR suffix (`/24`) tells your machine. What are the **private** address ranges, and what does **NAT** do to a packet on its way out and its way back? Why does a web server log an address that appears nowhere on your laptop — and what did NAT make hard?
5. "The network is slow" is three separate claims. Define **latency**, **bandwidth**, and **loss** (plus **jitter**), and give a physical cause for each. Using Week 1's latency hierarchy, explain why 20 **sequential** requests at 50 ms per round trip take a full second regardless of link speed. Then explain why a host that doesn't answer `ping` is not necessarily down.

#### Part B — How your machine and your Python show you this

6. What problem do **ports** solve that IP addresses don't? Explain the **well-known** and **ephemeral** ranges, and the **four-tuple** (source address, source port, destination address, destination port) that identifies one connection. Why can't two programs listen on the same port, while one server can hold thousands of connections on a single port — and why does your *outbound* request also consume a port?
7. What's the difference between listening on `127.0.0.1`, on `0.0.0.0`, and on one specific interface address? What is **loopback**, and does traffic to it reach the NIC at all? Use all of that to explain "it works on my machine but nothing else can reach it" — and why your machine's **hostname is not an address**.
8. A **socket is a file descriptor** (Week 5). What does that buy you in practice, which OS calls therefore treat it like a file, and what does that have to do with `lsof`? What is the OS doing while your program sits blocked in `recv()`, and what exactly is a **timeout** protecting you from?
9. One level above the packet: what does **TCP** add on top of best-effort delivery, and what does **UDP** deliberately refuse to add? Name one thing each is the right choice for. (Weeks 11–12 go deep — here, just state the contract.)
10. *(Tie-back to Weeks 1, 3, 5, and 6.)* Trace `urllib.request.urlopen("http://example.com")` all the way out and back. Name what happens at each step: the **name** turned into an address (Week 10's job), the **socket** the OS creates and the **file descriptor** it returns (Week 5), the source and destination **address and port** chosen, the **packets** leaving the NIC, the **hops** across the internet, the reply packets, the **bytes** copied into your process (Week 2), and the **object** now sitting in RAM with a name bound to it (Week 3). Then name one realistic way each step fails.

### Practical Tasks

Each task is a separate, tiny artifact (think 5–20 lines of Python — or, for Task 5, a saved command transcript). Run each one, look at the output, and write one sentence in your notes about what it showed you. Each task practices something from the questions above. **Task 6 is optional** — a stretch for when you want to go further.

1. **Task 1 — Every address this machine has.** *(Practices Q2, Q4, and Q7.)* Using `psutil.net_if_addrs()` and `psutil.net_if_stats()` (the same `psutil` from Week 1 — `pip install psutil` if it isn't in your current environment), print one block per interface: the **MAC** (family `psutil.AF_LINK`), the **IPv4** address with its **netmask**, any **IPv6** addresses, and whether the interface is up plus its **MTU**. Find `lo0` and `en0` in the output. Two details are worth a sentence each, because both are lessons: loopback's MTU is enormous (~16384) next to `en0`'s 1500 — there's no wire and therefore no frame size to respect — and `speed` reads `0` on macOS, because the OS simply won't tell you. **Concepts:** `psutil.net_if_addrs()`, `psutil.net_if_stats()`, `socket.AF_INET` / `AF_INET6`, `psutil.AF_LINK`, netmask, MTU.
2. **Task 2 — Which address does the world see?** *(Practices Q4 and Q7.)* Three short parts. **(a)** Ask the OS which **source address** it would use to reach the internet: open a `SOCK_DGRAM` socket, `connect(("8.8.8.8", 80))` — a UDP `connect()` sends no packets at all, it just makes the OS consult the routing table — and print `getsockname()`. Note the high **ephemeral** port it also picked. **(b)** Fetch `https://api.ipify.org` and print the address the *server* saw. Compare the two: your private address, its public one — that gap is NAT. **(c)** Try the textbook idiom `socket.gethostbyname(socket.gethostname())` and watch it raise `gaierror` on macOS, because your `.local` hostname isn't in DNS. **Concepts:** `SOCK_DGRAM`, `connect()` / `getsockname()`, ephemeral port, `urllib.request.urlopen()`, NAT, `socket.gaierror`.
3. **Task 3 — Bind a port, then find it in the OS's tables.** *(Practices Q6, Q7, and Q8.)* Bind a TCP socket to `127.0.0.1:8099`, call `listen()`, print `getsockname()` and **`fileno()`**, then park the script on `input("press enter to release the port... ")`. While it waits, in a second terminal run `lsof -nP -iTCP:8099` and `netstat -an -p tcp | grep 8099`. Look at `lsof`'s **FD** column: it's the same small integer your script printed — Week 5's file descriptor, now with an address attached. (macOS writes the address as `127.0.0.1.8099`, with a dot before the port.) Then, still holding it, run a second copy of the script and read the error you get. **Concepts:** `socket.bind()` / `listen()`, `getsockname()`, `fileno()`, `lsof -i`, `netstat -an`, `EADDRINUSE` (errno 48).
4. **Task 4 — Time a round trip honestly.** *(Practices Q5.)* Resolve one host to an IP **once** with `socket.getaddrinfo()`, then time five `socket.create_connection()` calls **to that IP** (`timeout=2`) with `time.perf_counter()`, printing min/avg/max in milliseconds. Do it for a nearby host and a distant one, then print how long **20 sequential** round trips would cost at your measured average. Two things to look for: if you connect by *name* instead, your first sample is badly inflated because it includes the name lookup (~190 ms vs ~65 ms after, on a real run) — which is exactly why you resolve first; and set your result next to Week 1's numbers, where one round trip buys you about a million RAM accesses. **Concepts:** `socket.getaddrinfo()`, `socket.create_connection()`, `timeout=`, `time.perf_counter()`, RTT, N × RTT.
5. **Task 5 — Follow the path out of your house.** *(Practices Q3 and Q5 — a shell task; save it as `solutions/task5_follow_the_path.md`.)* Run four commands and paste each one's real output with a couple of sentences on what it proved: `netstat -rn -f inet | head` (find the `default` row — that's your gateway and the interface it leaves by), `route -n get default` (the same fact plus the MTU), `ping -c 3 <your-gateway>`, and `traceroute -n -q 1 -w 1 example.com`. Note which hop is your own router, where the latency jumps, and where stars appear. Expect two surprises, each worth a sentence: your gateway may **ignore `ping` while still answering traceroute's hop 1** — filtered ICMP is not a down host, and mistaking the two is a classic false alarm — and some hops will show `10.x` **private** addresses, because Q4's private ranges are in use inside your ISP's own network too. **Concepts:** routing table, default gateway, hop, TTL, `traceroute`, filtered ICMP.
6. **Task 6 — Loopback vs the wire, and a packet nobody catches.** *(Optional — practices Q1, Q7, and Q9.)* Two parts. **(a)** Take `psutil.net_io_counters(pernic=True)` before and after fetching a URL, and print the per-interface **deltas** — the bytes show up on `en0`. Now do it again while sending a few kilobytes to Task 3's socket on `127.0.0.1:8099`: `lo0` moves and `en0` doesn't, which is loopback proving it never touches the hardware. **(b)** Open a `SOCK_DGRAM` socket with a 1-second timeout, `connect()` it to a port nothing is listening on, and `send()` a few bytes: the send **succeeds** — best-effort delivery hands out no receipts — and only the *following* `recv()` raises `ConnectionRefusedError`, because an ICMP port-unreachable came back after the fact. Compare that with TCP, which refuses at once. **Concepts:** `psutil.net_io_counters(pernic=True)`, loopback vs NIC, `SOCK_DGRAM`, best-effort delivery, `ConnectionRefusedError` (errno 61), `settimeout()`.
